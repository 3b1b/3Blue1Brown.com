If you haven't seen it already, I highly recommend looking at the [blackboard lecture](https://youtu.be/oIk3R-sMX5o) on chip design from the CEO of MatX, Reiner Pope. Some of you may also know him as an author on the [Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102) paper, and while learning more I also really enjoyed [this conversation](https://youtu.be/qvrdCpLPbuQ) he had with John Collison. The takeaway from all of these is not just that they're making chips designed for modern LLMs, but that the way they build is rooted in deep understanding and first-principles thinking. One apt quote Reiner has here is that "a few days of thinking can be worth more than weeks of simulation."

If you're the kind of person who thrives on understanding the full stack, from logic gates up to attention heads, there are not a lot of places where you can engage with that under one roof. This is _especially_ true if you also want to be part of a lean and highly leveraged team. I know many viewers here cherish a deep understanding and getting to the bottom of what's really going on. What's very pleasing is to work on something that requires that level of depth.

The featured post on [future leakage in block-quantized attention](https://matx.com/research/leaky_quantization) offers a good example here, because the relevant bug is actually very subtle. A later token's magnitude changes how an earlier token gets rounded, and information quietly flows backward through a causal mask. It's the kind of thing you only notice if you understand not just the model, but the underlying numerics. More generally, a lot of hardware performance improvement can come from low-precision arithmetic, but this immediately raises the question of when throwing away bits can work, and when it bites you.

The open roles span silicon, rack design, compilers, kernels, and ML models. If any of that interests you, do check it out.
