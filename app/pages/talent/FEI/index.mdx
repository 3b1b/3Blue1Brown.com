## Select Examples of Our Work

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) lets programs run on encrypted data without ever decrypting the inputs or intermediate values. Using just polynomial arithmetic (with a dash of circuit engineering cleverness), we can solve surprisingly complex problems without knowing anything about the input. Combining this with a carefully designed software system optimized for parallel computing, we've built things from Fog of War chess to a Connect Four engine and more — all fast enough that you can barely tell anything different is happening under the hood.

### Fog of War Chess

Fog of War chess is a chess variant in which each player sees only the squares their pieces can move to. Normally that takes a central server that knows both players' pieces and calculates what each side can see. Under FHE, clever board and piece encodings enable each side's vision to be computed entirely on encrypted data. No one sees the full board until the game ends, and in some implementations no server is needed at all, as the encrypted calculations can run in a player's browser.

<YouTube id="SoC6RaiTgvs" thumbnail={fogOfWarChess} />

### Connect Four

In Connect Four, the first player can always force a win, but the winning strategies involve a lot of branching, which is hard under FHE because every operation must be data-oblivious. Instead, the engine precomputes a set of early-game moves and selects among them with an encrypted one-hot board state until the board reaches a steady state. From there it tracks that state and plays by a small set of rules instead of memorized moves.

<YouTube id="HhCliaKedZs" thumbnail={connectFour} />

### Multiparty LUPI

In LUPI (Lowest Unique Positive Integer), everyone picks a number, and the lowest number nobody else picked wins. This demo runs it on multiparty FHE, so there is no trusted party at all. Before play, the keyholders run distributed key generation in their browsers, and the group key they end up with has no single owner — 5 of the 6 keyholders must combine their shares of the decryption key to reveal a result, and the server never holds any share of it. At one demo, 27 players on various devices all played over the same network.

### The Math Behind FHE

These lecture notes explain how the CKKS scheme uses ring theory to construct fully homomorphic encryption. The security of CKKS relies on the ring learning with errors (RLWE) assumption: roughly, that adding a small amount of noise to an equation of ring elements makes it hard to solve. CKKS encrypts data by representing batches of complex numbers in terms of ring elements and adding a small amount of noise, making it possible to do approximate arithmetic on encrypted data in a single-instruction multiple-data format.
